Writing is a lonely business. The act requires hours, upon hours of solitude spent labouring to create, revise and edit a story. Many writers feel isolated knowing that those in their lives don’t understand their pursuit of this craft. Writing changes you, and writers experience the world differently. We reflect, we observe the tiniest details, and get hung up on the shape and meaning of things. Many people think they can write, think it's easy, until they sit down to do it themselves. Many who start to write a book will give up, and talent is no indication of success or even staying power.

As a parent, I've heard the adage: It takes a village to raise a child'. It’s true, and as a writer, I know that a supportive village can help with nurturing a book. Writing is about sharing experience, and experience is enhanced through belonging to a community.

It takes a village to raise a child... a supportive village can help nurture a book.

Today, there's no shortage of ways you can communicate. I'm part of a vibrant, welcoming and fun [writing community on Twitter](https://twitter.com/search?q=%23WritingCommunity&src=tyah). There are similar experiences to be found on Facebook, with writing groups catering to almost every genre and sub-genre imaginable. I've formed terrific friendships with other writers all over the world -- people who understand the craft, and the effort, and all too often, the heartache of pursuing this vocation. Social media has proved invaluable to me, as I have struggled to find local writing support groups, even in a city as large as Melbourne.

Yet, I was reminded today of my earliest years as a writer -- before Twitter, Facebook, and even before I had the guts to start a blog, much less publish a book. Last night, a young man aspiring to blog and write novels reached out to me asking to talk about writing. He learnt of me through his mother, who briefly worked with my wife. I was both humbled and delighted by the chance to pay forward not only my successes but to honour those who had helped me. The early years were a struggle, filled with disappointments and setbacks, as I took my first steps on this uncertain path. The pain was lessened by those around me. Community is important, yet it's easy to overlook those communities that don't reside in a virtual world of 1s and 0s, and that's a shame.

>The pain was lessened by those around me.

In my early twenties, I went through something of a crisis. For much of my youth, I aspired to an academic career, but halfway through my undergraduate degree, I knew it wasn't for me. My true calling was writing, a feeling that bloomed slowly from my teenage years until it burned with a fury I couldn't quench. It wasn't easy admitting this, and I fought against it longer than I should have. It’s the people in my life who helped me negotiate those difficult days.

I've never been mentored, and part of me wonders what that would have been like. Yet, I can name those figures in formative years who've made an impression, offered encouragement, given praise when merited, and support when needed. Authors I've met, such as [Margaret Geddes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Geddes) and [Michael Pryor](http://www.michaelpryor.com.au/), and even the late and dearly missed Sir Terry Pratchett. Scholars like Barry Collett, Ann Trindade, Jenny Lee and Mark Davis. The spheres of community are vital to that; these were people I met through personal connections, at university, or by taking opportunities when they presented themselves, and I'm grateful beyond words for my experiences. 

A lot's happened to me and the publishing industry since I shared a meal with Margaret Geddes in my mother-in-law’s house many years ago. I gained and lost representation by literary agent [Jacinta di Mase](https://www.jacintadimase.com.au/). I lived abroad and travelled the world. I watched in astonishment as Amazon made traditional publishing obsolete overnight. I've become a husband and dad. I've built a successful, professional writing career. My blog gets more visitors than I had any right to expect, and I've published two fantasy books, with my third and fourth well on the way.

What's not changed is the need for community in every endeavour. Large or small, virtual or face-to-face. I've made valuable friendships the world over: writers and readers alike, and from the most unexpected quarters, far removed from Australia's shores. Without them, without that community, I'd have given up.

>What's not changed is the need for community.

My parting thoughts are simple: embrace community. If you are starting, don't be afraid to reach out. If you're well on your way, don't be a douche and pull up the ladder behind you -- grab hold when someone reaches out. Kindness and encouragement go a long way.
